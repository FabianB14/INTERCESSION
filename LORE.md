# SESSION — Story Bible

Reference document. Everything written for this game — prop labels, audio logs, UI copy, room names — must be consistent with what follows. When in doubt about tone, err toward clinical understatement. The Institute never sounds evil. It sounds helpful.

---

## The premise in one paragraph

In 1971, Dr. Alan Verity opened a private clinic in a converted county annex outside the city. He believed grief was not a wound to be talked through but a **lock** — a mechanism with a correct sequence — and that memory was the key. His treatment, the Session, placed a patient inside a physical reconstruction of the day they lost someone, and told them they could leave once they had worked it out. The Institute closed in 1984. The rooms did not stop.

---

## Dr. Alan Verity

Not a monster. That's the point.

A grief counselor who lost his wife and daughter in the same year and could not talk himself out of it. He built the first room in his own basement, for himself, and it worked — or he believed it did, which for our purposes is the same thing. Everything he did afterward came from wanting to give other people the thing that had saved him.

His voice is on the intake tapes. Warm, unhurried, faintly apologetic about the paperwork. He should be the most likeable thing in the game.

The horror is not that Verity was cruel. It's that he was **right about the mechanism and wrong about the cost**, and the building learned the mechanism better than he did.

**Verity's Three Principles** (stenciled above the intake desk, appears on every form footer):

> I. The room is honest.
> II. The room is patient.
> III. No room may be left unfinished.

The third one is the rule that kills people.

---

## The Protocol

What a patient's day looked like, and what the players are unknowingly repeating:

1. **Intake.** Personal effects surrendered. A questionnaire, deceptively gentle, that establishes what the room will be built from.
2. **Placement.** The patient enters a reconstruction. It is not symbolic. It is furniture, wallpaper, and light, assembled from the questionnaire and from things the patient never wrote down.
3. **Work.** The room contains a sequence. Solve it and the door opens. Verity called the sequence "the honest question."
4. **Release.** The patient exits and is walked to the next room. Attendants escort. Attendants do not speak during escort.
5. **Discharge.** After the final room. Nobody in the Institute's records was ever discharged.

---

## What went wrong

Verity's error was assuming the room was a tool he was holding.

By 1979 the reconstructions were arriving faster than his staff could stage them, and more accurate than the questionnaires could account for. Rooms began including objects the patient had never disclosed. Staff notes from this period shift from clinical to careful. One nurse's memo, unsigned: *"It is building ahead of us now. I do not think we should let it see the new intakes."*

The Institute closed after a night the records call the Overrun. There is no account of what happened. There are forty-one intake files and no discharge papers.

## The Attendant

Not a ghost. Not a demon. **Staff.**

It wears the Institute's orderly uniform because that is what the building understands an enforcer to look like. It is the third principle given a body. It does not care that players are frightened, does not care that they are trespassing, does not care that they are not patients. It cares that a room was left unfinished.

- It **walks.** Always. Players can hear where it is.
- It **speaks**, rarely, in the language of a form letter. "You've stepped out of session." "We'll go back now." Never a threat, never a snarl.
- It **cannot enter a room that is still being worked.** Stay and solve, and you are safe. The moment you try to skip, you're in the hallway with it. Every horror beat in this game should push players toward the mistake of leaving.

Design rule: the Attendant must be **learnable**. A player on their fourth run should be able to explain its rules to a new player in thirty seconds. Fear should come from breaking rules under pressure, not from not knowing them.

---

## Why the players see different rooms

They went in together. The building took intake on each of them separately.

The reconstruction each player stands in is assembled from their own history, which is why the woman in the corner is a hospital privacy curtain to one player and a shower curtain in a childhood bathroom to another. The **objects are the same objects.** The Institute is not lying to anyone. It is showing each person the honest version.

This is the in-fiction reason players must describe things aloud to each other, and it is the reason the game is frightening in a way that survives the jump scares: you cannot trust your own eyes, only your friends' voices.

---

## Tone rules

- **The Institute is never evil in its own voice.** Signage is helpful. Forms are polite. Verity is kind. The dread comes from the gap between the tone and what's actually happening.
- **No gore as a first resort.** The scariest asset in this game is a correctly filled-out form with a player's name already on it.
- **Never explain the building.** Players should leave with a working theory, not an answer. Verity himself did not know what he'd made — his final dictation should make that clear and should not be a reveal, just a man going quiet.
- **Avoid mental-illness-as-monster.** The patients are victims of a treatment, never the threat. Any writing that makes a patient frightening *because* they were grieving gets cut.

---

## Content that is cheap to make and carries the most story

Prioritize in this order for Early Access:

1. **Intake tapes** (Verity's voice). One VO actor, a good mic, a room's worth of blankets. Highest story-per-dollar asset in the project.
2. **Patient files.** Text on paper props. Nearly free. Establish the forty-one names, use the same names in room details, let players connect them.
3. **Staff memos.** The 1979–1984 drift from clinical to frightened. Tells the whole collapse without a cutscene.
4. **Doorframe tallies.** Environmental storytelling, one decal, reused everywhere.
5. **The Overrun.** Hold this back. It is the Early Access to 1.0 hook.

## Names in use

Keep a running registry here so no name is reused inconsistently.

- Dr. Alan Verity — founder
- The Verity Institute for Applied Recollection — full legal name, appears on letterhead
- The Session — the treatment
- The Protocol — the five-step procedure
- The Attendant — the enforcer
- The Overrun — the closure event, 1984
- Rooms are numbered, never named, in all signage: **Room 9**, **Room 17**. Players will name them themselves. Let them.
